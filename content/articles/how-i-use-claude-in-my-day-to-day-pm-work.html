Think about the best junior you ever trained. For the first month they were bright but generic. Then they learnt the house standards, and you could hand them a meeting recording, say “minutes, please”, and stop re-checking the output. Their intelligence never changed. Their skills did.

Artificial intelligence follows the same rule as human intelligence. Claude arrives clever, and it arrives knowing nothing about how your organisation works. The value appears when you teach it your standards, and the product now has features built for exactly that purpose. I audited my own usage recently and found that four habits carry almost all the weight, so this piece walks through those four.

1. Projects

A Project in Claude is a workspace with two things attached: custom instructions and a set of documents. Every chat opened inside it inherits both. That kills the ritual of opening each conversation with three paragraphs of background.

My rule for what goes where is simple. Instructions hold whatever rarely changes, meaning the product, its users and what your internal vocabulary means. Knowledge holds the material that evolves, such as the current spec, the decision log and the metrics glossary. The chat then only needs to carry today’s question.

The habit that compounds is writing back. When a chat produces a decision or a finding, I save it into the project, so the next chat starts from there. The setup took me fifteen minutes, and every conversation after it begins already briefed.

2. Skills

Here the junior-colleague comparison stops being a comparison, because the feature is literally called Skills. A skill is a folder of instructions that Claude loads on its own when a task matches it. You write the standard once and it applies every time after.

My minutes format is strict: numbered discussion points, one sentence per line, fixed naming for every team. It used to cost me a paragraph of explanation per meeting. It now lives in a skill, so my entire prompt has become “Give MoM”. Those two words replace the whole explanation.

My document skill goes further. It carries the house style and a pre-delivery checklist: cross-references resolve, the contents page matches the headings, every constraint names the mechanism that enforces it. Quality control happens during drafting instead of after review.

A PM could build the same for specs, release notes, research synthesis or the weekly update. The habit that matters more than the setup: when the output misses, fix the skill, not just the output. That correction becomes permanent.

3. Prompts and Cowork

The standard prompting advice holds: give context, show an example of good output, state the audience and format. Write the brief you would give a contractor who has never met your company.

What my audit actually surfaced is that my prompts keep getting shorter. The context moved into projects. The standards moved into skills. What is left to type is only what is new today.

Illustrative counts for one recurring deliverable of mine.

The other half of this habit is knowing when to leave the chat window. I use the chat for thinking and Cowork for the production work. Cowork is Claude’s agentic mode: hand it a folder of transcripts and a task, and it comes back with the finished spreadsheet or document rather than a description of one. It can also run on a schedule. This article, including every image in it, came out of one Cowork session.

4. Scenarios

The use I value most per hour spent is rehearsal. Before a difficult review I give Claude the document and a role: you are the head of risk, sceptical and precise. Raise your ten hardest objections. Then I practise answers until they stop being bad. The real meeting never matches the rehearsal exactly. It does not need to.

Two variants earn their keep. The pre-mortem: it is six months from now and this launch failed. Write the honest post-mortem. And the slip: UAT moves by two weeks. Show me the three least painful recovery paths and their cost. Ten minutes of either converts a future panic into a note waiting in a drawer.

My guess about where this goes is that the mechanical layer of PM work, the assembling and formatting and consolidating, gets absorbed, and the job concentrates into judgement. Clever phrasing alone will not take you there. What works is the same approach you would take with a junior colleague. Teach it your standards, and rehearse the difficult days together before they arrive.
