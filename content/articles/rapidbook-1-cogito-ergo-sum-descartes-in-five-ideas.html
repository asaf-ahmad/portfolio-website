What does “I think, therefore I am” mean? A plain summary of Descartes’s cogito ergo sum in five ideas, with the objections that still stand.

Most of us have woken from a dream and needed a few seconds to work out that it did not happen. For those seconds, the dream was as convincing as the room we woke up in. René Descartes (1596–1650), a French mathematician and philosopher, took that small experience seriously. He asked how he could be sure of anything at all. His answer became the most quoted line in philosophy, “cogito, ergo sum”, or “I think, therefore I am”. He first stated it in Discourse on the Method (1637) and argued it in full in Meditations on First Philosophy (1641). Here is the argument in five ideas.

1. Doubt as a tool

Descartes did not doubt because he was gloomy or because he enjoyed an argument. He doubted because he wanted knowledge that could not collapse later. He compared his beliefs to a house built on weak foundations, and he decided to pull the whole house down and start again.

In the first of his six Meditations, Descartes tests his beliefs in three steps. Each step is harsher than the one before.

  1. He notes that his senses sometimes deceive him, so he cannot trust them completely.
  2. He points out that he cannot always tell dreaming from waking. Even “I am sitting by the fire” is therefore open to doubt.
  3. He imagines a powerful, malicious demon who feeds him false experiences. The demon could even make him go wrong when he adds two and three.

Descartes did not believe the demon existed. He used it as a test. If a belief could survive even the demon, he could rely on it completely.

Many readers get this part wrong about him. Descartes was not a sceptic who thought nothing could be known. He treated doubt the way an engineer treats a stress test. He applied the heaviest load he could think of, and he watched for what was still standing afterwards.

2. What cogito ergo sum means

At the start of the second Meditation, Descartes has almost nothing left. He has doubted the world, his own body and even mathematics. Then he notices something. To be deceived, he has to exist. To doubt, he has to be thinking. The demon can fool him about everything except the fact that there is someone being fooled.

That is the whole of the cogito. Every time Descartes thinks “I exist”, the thought proves itself, because something has to be doing the thinking.

Two details are worth knowing. The first is the wording. Descartes wrote “je pense, donc je suis” in French in the Discourse in 1637. The Latin “cogito, ergo sum” appears in his Principles of Philosophy in 1644. In the Meditations he puts it differently. There he says that “I am, I exist” is true whenever he says it or thinks it.

The second detail is what the line does not claim. Descartes is not saying that thinking causes him to exist. He is also not saying that things which cannot think are unreal. His claim is narrower. His own existence is the one fact he can know for certain from the inside, at the moment he thinks it.

3. A thing that thinks

Having shown that he exists, Descartes asks what he is. He cannot answer “a man with a body”, because he has not yet shown that bodies are real. All he can say is that he is a thing that thinks. By thinking he means a great deal. Doubting, understanding, wanting, refusing, imagining and feeling all count.

He then picks up a piece of beeswax to make a point about knowledge. Fresh from the hive, the wax is hard and cold, and it still smells of flowers. Held near the fire, it melts, and every one of those qualities changes. Yet Descartes still judges it to be the same wax. His senses did not tell him that, because everything they reported has changed. His mind told him. He concludes that we know even physical things through understanding more than through sight and touch.

I find this idea useful in a way Descartes did not intend. People who lose a job or leave a long career often say they no longer know who they are. On Descartes’s account, the “I” was never the job title, or anything else that someone can take away. The “I” is the one doing the thinking, and that part is still there on Monday morning.

4. Rebuilding with four rules

Doubt was only half of the project. Descartes wanted to rebuild, and in Part Two of the Discourse he gives himself four rules for doing it.

  1. Accept nothing as true unless it is so clear that there is no reason to doubt it.
  2. Divide every difficulty into as many parts as possible.
  3. Start with the simplest things and move step by step to the complex ones.
  4. Make reviews so complete that nothing is left out.

He trusted these rules because they were how mathematicians worked, and mathematics was the one subject where he found certainty.

The rules are nearly four hundred years old and they still describe good practice. A programmer hunting a bug follows them without knowing it. The second rule is also the best advice I know for a decision that feels too large. Someone who asks a forum “should I quit my job?” is really asking several smaller questions about money, health, skills and what comes next. Strangers cannot answer the large question. The person asking can usually answer the small ones, one at a time.

In the Meditations, Descartes rebuilds his own beliefs in the same spirit. He argues from the cogito to the existence of God, and from God to a world he can trust.

5. Where Descartes went wrong

Very few philosophers today accept the whole of Descartes’s system. I think the objections are part of what makes him worth reading.

The first problem is the rebuild. Descartes says he can trust whatever he perceives clearly and distinctly, because God exists and does not deceive. But he proves that God exists by relying on clear and distinct ideas. Antoine Arnauld, a theologian of his own day, pointed out that this looks like arguing in a circle.

The second problem is the split between mind and body. Descartes held that the mind is a thinking thing with no size or shape, while the body is a physical machine. In 1643, Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia wrote to ask him how something with no physical properties could move an arm. Descartes never gave her an answer that satisfied her, and I do not think he had one.

Some critics go after the cogito itself. In the eighteenth century, Georg Lichtenberg suggested that Descartes was entitled to say only “there is thinking”, and not “I think”.

What survives is the starting point. Descartes made it normal to ask what we can know before we ask what is true, and to begin the answer with our own experience.

This summary leaves out most of his argument for God, which runs through the third and fifth Meditations and deserves a careful reading of its own. The Meditations is a short book, and you can read the first two in an evening. If the dream question stays with you this week, that is where I would start.
