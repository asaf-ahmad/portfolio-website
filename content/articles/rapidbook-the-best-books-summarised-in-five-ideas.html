The best books in five ideas each, for people who own more books than they have time to read.

There is a book on my bedside table with a boarding pass tucked in at page 43. The flight was in March. I have not read page 44. I bought the book because three people I respect told me it changed how they think, and I still believe them. I have not got back to it.

I suspect you own a book like that too. RapidBook is my attempt to do something useful about it. It is a series of short book summaries, and this first article explains what the series is and what you can expect from it.

1. One book, five ideas

Each RapidBook article takes one important book and reduces it to five ideas. Every idea gets about 200 words of explanation. The whole article takes five or six minutes to read, which is roughly the time you spend waiting for a cab.

I chose that format after noticing how I remember books. A year after finishing one, I do not remember chapters. I remember a handful of ideas, and usually one example that made each idea stick. Most of the 300 pages did the work of persuading me, and persuasion fades once the idea has landed. So RapidBook starts from what survives.

Five is a limit I set on purpose. With ten points, a summary becomes a list of contents, and you close it remembering none of them. With five, I have to decide what the book is really about, and that decision is the hard part of the work. If I cannot find five ideas worth 200 words each, the book does not belong in the series.

2. The best books from different areas

RapidBook is not a business series or a philosophy series. I will pick the top books across several areas, including philosophy, psychology, money, science, history and work. The test for a book is simple. People should still be arguing about it, or quietly using it, long after it was published.

The mix matters because problems do not arrive labelled by subject. Someone deciding whether to quit a stable job faces a money question and a psychology question at once. Sometimes there is a philosophical question underneath as well, about what a good life is. A reader who knows only one shelf of the bookshop answers all of that with one tool.

The series starts with René Descartes and the line most people know him by. It is cogito, ergo sum, which means “I think, therefore I am”. He first wrote the thought in French, in his Discourse on the Method in 1637. I am starting there because Descartes asks the question that sits under every other book. How do I know that what I believe is true? That felt like the right foundation before moving on to habits, markets and minds.

3. Ideas tied to ordinary life

A summary that only restates the author is a shorter version of a book you were not reading anyway. So wherever it fits, I will connect an idea to a situation you might recognise.

Take Descartes. His method was to doubt everything he had been taught until he reached something he could not doubt. That sounds remote from daily life until you look at the questions people post online. On any career forum you will find someone asking whether they are in the wrong job. The replies come from a crowd of confident strangers who disagree with each other. Descartes would not tell that person what to do. He would ask which beliefs about the job they have tested themselves, and which they absorbed from parents, colleagues or the internet. That is a usable question, and it is nearly four hundred years old.

I will not force this. Some ideas are worth knowing because they are true or beautiful, and bolting a life lesson onto them would be dishonest. Where an example helps, you will get one. Where it does not, I will explain the idea and move on.

4. How RapidBook helps you

The series helps in different ways, depending on where you stand with a book.

If you have not read the book, a RapidBook article helps you decide whether to. Five ideas are enough to tell whether a book speaks to a problem you have now. That is a better filter than a bestseller list. It also costs you six minutes instead of the price of a book that ends up holding a boarding pass.

If you read the book years ago, the article works as a refresher. Most of us finish a book, feel changed for a fortnight and then lose the details. Going back over the central ideas brings a surprising amount of it back.

There is also the reader who will never open the original, and I would rather be honest about that than pretend otherwise. Not everyone needs to read Descartes in full. Knowing what he argued, and why it still matters, is useful when his name comes up in a conversation, an interview or another book. You will know what is being referred to, and you can decide for yourself whether to go deeper.

5. What a book summary cannot do

A summary is a map, and I do not want to oversell it. Five ideas in 200 words each cannot carry an author’s full argument, the objections they answered or the pleasure of their writing. Descartes builds his case step by step across many pages, and the building is part of the point. When I compress that, something is lost. I will say so when the loss is large.

So I would suggest using RapidBook in a particular way. Read the article first. If one of the five ideas stays with you for a few days, that is your signal to buy the book and read it properly. If nothing stays, you have saved yourself a purchase and learnt what the book says anyway.

I will also keep my own opinion visible. When I think an author was wrong, or when later thinkers have largely moved past a claim, I will tell you plainly. A summary that only admires its subject is an advertisement, and you can get those on the back cover.

The first full article will cover Descartes and cogito, ergo sum. If there is a book you have been meaning to read for years, tell me in the responses and I will add it to the list. I still intend to finish the one on my bedside table.
