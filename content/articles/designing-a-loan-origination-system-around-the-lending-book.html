How I explain a lending book to a bank before anyone configures its loan origination system, in five ideas with a mind map.

The first time a credit head asked me to "just configure the LOS for our SME products", I drew the lending book on a whiteboard before touching a single screen. Term loans on one side, overdrafts in the middle, letters of credit and guarantees off to the right, and a thin strip along the bottom for the process every product passed through. That drawing has outlived three implementations. It is the shape I still use when I walk a bank through loan origination system design, and it is the shape of this article. Five ideas, in the order I would take a new product manager through them.

1. Two kinds of exposure

Every facility in a lending book either moves money today or promises to move it later. A term loan, a housing loan, a working capital demand loan or an overdraft puts funds in the borrower's hands, so the bank carries funded exposure from the day of disbursement. A letter of credit or a bank guarantee does something different. The bank promises to pay if documents comply or if a counterparty fails, and no money leaves until that happens.

I treat this as the first split in any loan origination system design because the two families need different machinery. Fund-based products need disbursement logic, utilisation tracking and repayment schedules. Non-fund-based products need limit setup for contingent utilisation, validity dates, commission handling and a plan for devolution, which is the moment a guarantee is invoked and a contingent exposure turns into a funded one overnight.

When a product manager skips this split, the symptoms show up late. Someone tries to attach an amortisation schedule to a bank guarantee, or a letter of credit lands in a report of funded exposure and inflates the book. Name the two families on day one and most of those defects never get written.

2. Repayment pattern decides the mechanics

Inside the fund-based family, I sort products by how the customer uses and repays them rather than by their marketing names. Three patterns cover almost everything I have configured.

  1. A term loan has a fixed sanctioned amount, one or more disbursements, and a schedule. The schedule may amortise, pay a bullet at the end or balloon, and restructuring means changing the interest, the tenure or the schedule itself.
  2. An overdraft or cash credit is revolving. The customer draws and repays many times within a sanctioned limit, interest runs only on the utilised amount, and the facility needs its own account and class in the core system. Renewal and enhancement are the main events in its life.
  3. A card-like construct is also revolving but adds a card or reference number, a billing cycle, a due date and a statement.

Once the pattern is clear, the configuration questions answer themselves. A term loan needs schedule generation. An overdraft needs renewal rules and a validity date. A card needs a statement engine. I have watched teams argue for a week about whether a working capital demand loan is "really" a term loan. The pattern settles it in a minute. The bank disburses it once and the customer repays it on a schedule, so it behaves like one.

3. Collateral as a configurable master

Security is where product-specific code forks begin, so I fight hardest to keep it in configuration. Each piece of collateral carries four things the credit team has to record in the loan origination system. Its role, meaning primary security that backs the facility and drives loan-to-value, or secondary security that gives extra comfort. Its nature, tangible such as gold, a vehicle, property, machinery or inventory, or intangible such as a guarantee, a fixed deposit lien, assigned receivables or an insurance policy. Its owner, because borrower-owned, guarantor-owned and third-party collateral trigger different legal checks. And the allocation actions it supports, which in practice come down to three. Reallocate the borrower's existing collateral, reallocate the guarantor's, or bring in something entirely new.

A security type master holds all of that, with a margin or loan-to-value rule, a valuation method and a document checklist per type. A retail vehicle loan, an SME receivables facility and a bank guarantee backed by a cash margin then draw from the same master instead of three bespoke modules.

The payoff arrives during enhancements, renewals and shortfall remediation. Those events reuse the same allocation actions. If collateral lives in a master, the LOS already knows how to handle them.

4. One process spine, many triggers

Whatever the product, the application walks the same spine. Accounting mapping assigns the general ledger by portfolio and product. Onboarding captures the customer, the relationship, the first security and the pricing inputs. Underwriting applies the credit policy and checks collateral coverage. Approvals route through the authority matrix, and deviations loop back for rework when a case breaks a rule. The core banking or loan management system books the account and limit. Disbursement follows, once or in tranches, and then management begins and runs for the life of the facility.

Lifecycle events do not need a second spine. An additional loan re-enters at underwriting with the existing exposure as context. An overdraft renewal re-enters with fresh financials, stock statements and a valuation check, then extends the validity in the core system. An enhancement raises the sanctioned limit and rechecks coverage, pricing and conditions, and it may pull in extra collateral. A restructuring changes interest, tenure or schedule and must leave an audit trail because it rewrites cash flows.

What differs by product is the set of triggers and parameters. Stock statements for working capital, statement cycles for cards, expiry controls for guarantees. The spine stays. I design the spine first and hang the triggers on it afterwards.

5. Four layers in the LOS

When I turn the picture into a build, I use four layers and refuse to let them blur. The first is data. Customer, application, facility, account and limit, security with its valuation and charge, disbursement, deviation and approval record. The second is configuration masters. Product, general ledger mapping, security type, pricing and fees, the approval matrix, deviation rules and the document checklist. The third is workflow. Product-family flows, stage-wise validations, maker and checker controls, rework loops for deviations, and queue and SLA management. The fourth is integration. Account and limit booking, charge and lien creation, disbursement posting, amendments for renewals and enhancements, and reconciliation with error handling.

The functional specification then has an obvious table of contents. For every flow it names the actors, the entry criteria, the main and alternate paths, the fields, the validations, and the approval thresholds with their deviation routing. The controls section asks for maker and checker on key actions, versioning of schedules and valuations, a full audit trail on sanction changes, and turnaround tracking by stage.

The outputs I hand to an implementation team are equally plain. Product configuration sheets, a security and document matrix, an API mapping for the core system, and test scenarios for sanction, disbursement, renewal, enhancement and restructuring.

The whiteboard drawing is still the most useful artefact I own, and it fits on one page. If you are about to configure a loan origination system, draw your own version before you open the product master. The arguments it settles early are the ones that would otherwise cost you a release.
