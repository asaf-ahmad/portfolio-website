A summary of The Magic of Thinking Big by David Schwartz in five ideas: belief, excusitis, action, big thinking and turning defeat into victory.

In the preface to The Magic of Thinking Big, David J. Schwartz describes a sales meeting he once watched. A vice president brought his best salesman, Harry, up onto the platform. Harry had earned just under $60,000 that year, while the other representatives averaged $12,000. The vice president told the room that Harry was not five times smarter than they were, and that he had not worked five times harder. The difference, he said, was that Harry thought five times bigger.

Schwartz published the book in 1959 to explain that difference, and it is still in print. This summary of The Magic of Thinking Big covers its five most useful ideas.

1. Belief sets the ceiling

Schwartz starts with belief, and he separates it from wishing. Nobody, he says, can wish themselves into an executive suite. Belief works differently. A person who believes they can do a thing starts working out how, because belief, in his words, “triggers the mind to figure ways and means”.

He gives an example from a state highway department. The department invited engineering firms to design eight bridges, for a fee of $200,000. Seventeen of the firms were small. Sixteen of them looked at the size of the job and decided it was beyond them. The seventeenth had three engineers. It submitted a proposal and won the contract.

Schwartz also compares belief to a thermostat. A person who believes they are worth little sets the dial low. Their effort and their results then settle at that level, and other people take them at their own valuation. Disbelief, he adds, works just as hard. A doubting mind attracts reasons to support the doubt.

Most of us know a modern version of the sixteen firms. A job advertisement lists ten requirements, a capable person meets seven, and they never apply.

Belief, on Schwartz’s account, starts the work. It does not replace the work.

2. Excusitis and its four forms

Schwartz invents a word for the habit of explaining failure in advance. He calls it excusitis, “the failure disease”, and he names its four most common forms.

  1. Health. The person feels too unwell to try.
  2. Intelligence. The person feels they lack the brains.
  3. Age. The person feels too old, or too young.
  4. Luck. The person feels that other people get the breaks.

His answers are blunt. On intelligence, he argues that the thinking which guides a mind matters more than the size of it. On luck, he tells of a salesman who brought in a $112,000 order and was called lucky by a weaker colleague. The salesman had worked on that customer for months.

His best-known answer is on age. A trainee called Cecil, aged forty, thought he was too old to start again as a manufacturer’s representative. Schwartz asked him when a working life begins and when it ends. Cecil guessed twenty and seventy. Cecil had therefore used twenty of his fifty productive years, and thirty remained.

Anyone who reads career forums will recognise Cecil. The question “am I too old to change careers at 30?” comes up constantly, and then again at 35 and at 40. By Schwartz’s arithmetic, a thirty-year-old still has 80 per cent of their working life ahead.

3. Action cures fear

Schwartz does not tell frightened people that their fear is imaginary. He says fear is real, and that reassurance does not cure it. Action does. During the Second World War he watched Navy recruits who could not swim. Each had to jump from a board six feet above deep water, and many stood there terrified. The fear ended for each man once he was in the water.

His rule is that waiting feeds fear and action starves it. He quotes a paratrooper instructor who said the jump itself was not so bad. The waiting was what got to the men.

Schwartz applies the same logic to confidence. To think confidently, he says, a person must first act confidently. He gives five exercises.

  1. Be a front seater.
  2. Make eye contact.
  3. Walk 25 per cent faster.
  4. Speak up at every meeting.
  5. Smile big.

Each exercise is a small act that a nervous person would avoid, repeated until it stops feeling like a risk.

Schwartz also warns against waiting until conditions are perfect, because they never are. His advice is to start mechanically. One writer he describes sits down, picks up a pencil and writes anything at all until his mind catches up.

4. Big words, better company

For Schwartz, thinking big is a habit a person can practise, and he begins with vocabulary. He argues that people think in pictures, and that words produce the pictures. “We face a problem” leaves one picture in a listener’s mind. “We face a challenge” leaves another.

He then asks readers to see what a thing can be, and not only what it is. His example is an estate agent who sells run-down farms. The agent spent two days walking one neglected property and wrote three plans for it, including a riding stable. He sold buyers the plan, and he got a higher price than agents who sold only the acreage.

The second half of the idea is about surroundings. Schwartz calls a person’s environment their “mind food”. He tells readers to take advice from people who have succeeded, and to keep away from those who explain why a thing cannot be done. His phrase “go first class” covers goods as well as company. He would have a person own one good pair of shoes before three poor ones.

He also asks readers to describe themselves ten years from now, under the headings of work, home and social life. Without that picture, he says, a person is asking for a ticket without naming a destination.

5. Turning defeat into victory

Schwartz holds that successful people meet as many setbacks as anyone else. What sets them apart is how they respond. He describes three men who match on age, intelligence and background. The first is knocked down and stays down. The second gets to his knees and crawls away, careful never to risk a beating again. The third gets up, learns the lesson and goes forward.

His model for learning is the air crash investigator. After every crash, investigators collect the fragments and interview witnesses until they know the cause. Then they correct the fault in every aircraft of that type. Schwartz asks readers to study their own setbacks in the same way, and to salvage something from each one.

That takes some courage. The opera singer Risë Stevens lost an important audition early in her career, and she wanted to hear that the verdict was unfair. Her teacher told her to have the courage to face her faults. Stevens lay awake that night and worked out what she had to improve.

Schwartz adds one caution. Persistence alone is not enough. He tells of a writer who had written sixty-two stories and sold none, because he used the same approach every time. Schwartz’s rule is to keep the goal and change the method.

Source: David J. Schwartz, The Magic of Thinking Big (Prentice-Hall, 1959)
